# Development Meeting Summary

**Date:** March 31, 2026

---

## New Block Explorer Deployed

The infrastructure lead presented a newly deployed block explorer, estimated at around seventy percent feature complete, with some substrate-side features still missing. Built on a modified BlockScout layout and styled with the network's website fonts and colors, the explorer foregrounds the network's time features as its primary identity. The main page surfaces mesh quality (which can change every block), the number of active validators, mesh convergence state, total blocks, block time, unique wallets, and consensus time — the agreed mesh time used to write timestamps and verify transactions. The first validator to see a transaction enter the mempool timestamps it, and that timestamp is traceable back to the validator, with severe slashing for deliberate mis-timestamping; because correct timestamping is easy, mis-timestamping is treated as intentional rather than a malfunction.

## Fee Priority Queue

A central feature highlighted was the fee priority queue, described as the conclusion of months of iteration on the fee problem. As mempool transactions accumulate, the displayed queue depth and average weight rise, incentivizing users to choose higher fee tiers to have their transactions timestamped sooner. This does not change how long a transaction takes to mine, but it changes how quickly it is timestamped — relevant for obligations sensitive to ordering. The team noted that staying below roughly one hundred milliseconds of timestamping delay is advisable to avoid potential front-running, so users are incentivized to raise fees to remain in a safe range. A key property discussed: when the chain has little activity, users pay minimal fees, but as adoption grows and congestion occurs (for example during volatile market periods), collective fees rise dramatically. The fee-priority system can be disabled via governance without endangering the chain, though it remains enabled by default.

## Safety Deadline and Time Display

The explorer exposes the fifteen-second inclusion deadline as an enforced-by-default safety feature; disabling it would expose the chain to attack vectors, and it should remain enforced unless usage growth makes the deadline unreliable, at which point a more complex inclusion-enforcement system (potentially reintroducing the court mechanism) would be considered. The team judged the current approach should perform well for at least the first two or three years. Transaction detail views show two distinct time sources: the block time (truncated to milliseconds on the EVM side) and the timestamping time, which carries full mesh precision down to the nanosecond. An internal node mapping links EVM transaction hashes to their substrate versions to recover the true timestamp. This timestamp is what determines on-chain transaction ordering and is central to the network's value proposition.

## Staking, Power Token, and Faucet Flow

The lead demonstrated the developer section: anyone can claim test tokens, then stake and unstake the network's power token through the explorer's MetaMask integration. On the testnet, staking and the unlock waiting period are compressed (an unlock takes roughly a minute rather than the production two weeks). Unlocking requires two transactions — creating a request and later claiming, at which point the power token is burned. Substrate-wallet connection and governance participation through the explorer are not yet supported but were judged a nice-to-have rather than a must.

## Fee Structure and Validator Rewards

The team discussed what still needs resolving before production: a coherent fee structure. Fees must be collectible on two elements — transactions (rewarding validators) and the time service itself — and the team needs to decide whether these are flat fees, how users pay them, and where they apply. Currently there is no reward for mining blocks, timestamping, or processing transactions; substrate supports many of these reward mechanisms by default but they must be configured. The team discussed enabling block rewards plus transaction fees as the simplest starting point, with timestamping handled separately since it is not a native substrate feature.

A consensus emerged to ship a simple fee structure on the testnet, gather feedback from real users and partners on where incentives should lie, and refine from there — while recognizing that investors will require a fully fleshed-out fee structure before mainnet, since validators face meaningful hardware investment and will ask where their revenue comes from. The team noted that timestamping provides MEV protection that users are willing to pay for, suggesting an initial higher price (a fraction of the MEV savings) that is gradually lowered with community feedback to avoid a premature race to zero. A simple proof of concept — for example, an ERC-20 / USDC deployment emulating payment rails and proving transactions are timestamped, or a basic swap contract — was proposed as the simplest demonstration of the value thesis. The team also discussed validator incentives to publicly advertise and protect their RPC endpoints, so that timestamping naturally distributes across many nodes rather than concentrating on the core validators.

## Time Services and Cross-Chain Discussion

Forward-looking ideas discussed included selling future "time slots," a perpetual-futures market leveraging fair real-time ordering, and a "time bridge" allowing other chains to request timestamping or time proofs for time-sensitive contracts. The team noted that other chains operate at their own slower pace, limiting bridge usability, but agreed even a simple time bridge could create engagement; integration through existing oracle networks was also revisited as a way to offer higher-resolution time data. The team reiterated that bringing liquidity to a new chain is hard, so DEX-style applications remain future-oriented, and that operational costs for timestamping are low once validators have their hardware, though the entry cost of running a properly behaved validator node is significant given the mesh's strict node-quality expectations.

## Wallet, Data System, and Distribution

A parallel update covered pushing toward a framework release, bundling the work, and updating the data wallet to point at the network's implementations as a generic PKCS-12 wallet, including a lighter version that does not require carrying the full data package, with planned CLI and MCP support. The team reiterated the Proton-style thick-client pattern as the fundamental architecture for applications built on the network, with AI assisting UX and workflow to manage the inherent complexity of flexible, powerful tooling. The framework's role was described as adapting to whatever capabilities an agentic stack already has and deploying the rest, so users get a full feature set regardless of their stack. A peer-to-peer federated discovery network across data-system knowledge bases was raised as a possible future direction, structurally feasible but not on the near-term roadmap.

## Meeting Infrastructure and Other Threads

The team discussed an alternative to the current meeting platform built and tested by a team member, featuring per-participant speaker-diarized transcription pipelines aggregated into a single roster, rolling summaries, deliverable detection, and open-question tracking, with potential white-label and data-system integration. Other threads mentioned included a sponsored/rate-limited inference-access feature for an agent platform aimed at small-language-model users, an NFT-based account aggregation proposal to be reviewed later, and continued work on transport-layer and peer-to-peer connectivity between data-system instances. The team closed with a discussion of language and platform choices (notably the rationale for using Rust on edge systems) and a note that more material would be sent to the time-hardware vendor as testing closes.
