# Development Meeting Summary

**Date:** March 17, 2026

---

## Replacing the Court System with a Deadline Mechanism

The infrastructure lead reported extensive stress testing of the court system, which forces validators to include all transactions in a block. Testing revealed that more than about fifty transactions per court created problems: when a block producer commits a court, it must locate every referenced transaction by hash in its mempool and recompute hashes, and if a significant share of transactions have not yet propagated, the reconciliation step takes too long and the producer can miss the block. The root cause was traced to limitations of the gossip protocol and uncontrollable mempool propagation timing. The sweet spot of roughly five seconds per court and fifty transactions per court capped throughput at around ten transactions per second, which the team judged insufficient.

The team realized that the problematic scenario is rare and required artificially unstable test nodes to reproduce. Since transactions typically propagate network-wide within about five seconds, the lead replaced the heavy reconciliation machinery with a simpler five-second deadline for transaction inclusion. This removed roughly six thousand lines of code and passed the same stress tests, while a small, hard-to-trigger chance of a transaction failing remains. To preserve censorship resistance, anti-censorship safeguards stay in place and validators are heavily penalized for failing to include transactions, so dropped transactions should occur only by accident. The change is pushed into the main protocol with a marker allowing reversion to the old approach. Another participant emphasized the importance of carefully tuning this slashing mechanism and building worst-case scenarios so honest validators are not unfairly penalized.

## Scaling Discussion: Eventual Consistency and Elected Block Producers

The team discussed the tension between the mesh network's eventual-consistency model — where nodes can drop off and rejoin and are not all updated simultaneously — and the need for reliable transaction ordering. A proposal was raised that, once the network is large enough, a provable subset of validators known to hold the required transactions could be elected to produce a block, with nodes gossiping hashes of their ready mempool contents to signal readiness. This was agreed to be a sound future direction rather than an immediate implementation, since the problem it solves is rare on a healthy network.

## Solidity Temporal Precompile and Auction Demo

A precompile was added that exposes the current block's mesh timestamp to nanosecond precision from within Solidity. This was demonstrated through an auction smart contract that stores each bid's timestamp at mesh precision, illustrating how temporal ordering makes front-running and gaming the auction difficult. Smart contract developers on the network gain access to the timestamp of the transaction currently executing — not just the block timestamp — enabling contracts to reject transactions that were not included quickly enough or to behave based on transaction creation time. The lead described plans to let an AI agent attempt to front-run the auction with full access to the chain code and RPC, as a focused adversarial audit of the ordering system.

## Positioning and Revenue Strategy Exploration

The team discussed how to articulate the network's defensibility and revenue model for potential investors. The moat was framed around physics: the time mesh requires real hardware and validator coordination that cannot be faked in software, and replicating temporal ordering on an existing chain would amount to rebuilding an equivalent system from scratch. Potential business directions discussed at a high level included a decentralized time oracle or timestamping service, a temporal-ordering layer, possible integrations with existing oracle networks, and comparative analysis of how applications running on other chains would perform on this network. The team acknowledged that extending MEV protection to other chains is difficult because it would require migrating liquidity, so early adopters are more likely to be those interested in the time feature itself; a strong working prototype was identified as a prerequisite for serious partnership conversations.

## Agentic Operating System and Internal Tooling

A broader discussion covered the team's parallel work building an "agentic operating system" — a stack combining model runtimes, OS substrates with secure secret and key handling, a coordination layer, and UI tooling. The team is working toward running multiple agents internally and enabling external users, with plans involving Stripe and HTTP 402 payment flows, the goal being to generate recurring revenue that lends legitimacy and supports investor conversations. Emphasis was placed on security: controlling and safely halting agents, role-based access modeled on real organizational structures, internal key management, and resource isolation so one agent cannot starve others.

The team contrasted its approach with less secure single-loop assistant projects, arguing that persistent agent loops, session restart, and context management are already supported in its framework, and that production-grade multi-agent orchestration requires careful guardrails. Considerable discussion covered running smaller local models (in the 4B–90B parameter range) successfully for medium-to-long-horizon tasks, expanding usable context automatically, and the rapid pace of incorporating new research and models. The conversation closed with reflections on software development becoming a more deterministic, process-driven activity as tooling matures, while genuine innovation remains concentrated in research and proof-of-concept work.
